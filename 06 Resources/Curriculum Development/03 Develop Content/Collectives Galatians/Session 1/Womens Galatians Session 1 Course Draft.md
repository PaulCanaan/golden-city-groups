## Session Formation Emphasis

**Receive your identity and belonging from Christ’s grace, and practice truthful encouragement without making comparison or approval the measure of spiritual worth.** Paul reviewed the development Canvas synthesis and requested handouts from it. This pastoral emphasis remains specific to this session rather than a universal claim about female discipleship.

- **Belonging:** Create room for each woman’s actual life and questions without grading her by another person’s story or expectations.
- **Beholding:** Attend to Jesus’ self-giving rescue and the Father’s initiative in grace rather than constructing a comparative measure of acceptance.
- **Becoming:** Recognize one approval/comparison pattern and respond through prayer, gospel truth, and a concrete word or action of gracious encouragement.

This draft does not assume marriage, motherhood, caregiving, employment, a particular age, or a preferred emotional style. Comparison and self-reliance can affect anyone; the particular examples should be tested against the women’s real context.

## Teaching Block 1 — Grace Begins with What Christ Has Done

### 6:50–6:54 — Entering the Letter

**Draft teaching script**

We are beginning a letter about the gospel, freedom, and life together as God’s people. Paul writes to churches in Galatia, including Gentile believers who have come to faith in Christ. They are facing a message that asks whether receiving Christ is enough or whether additional qualifications are necessary to be accepted by God.

As the letter unfolds, we will encounter circumcision, shared meals, the promise to Abraham, and the relationship between the law and God’s promise. These are real questions in the churches’ historical situation. Jewish people and Jewish identity are not the enemy of the gospel. Paul is confronting the use of additional requirements as a basis for righteousness and belonging before God.

The churches need more than an inspiring message about feeling free. They need to understand who has acted to rescue them, what they have received in Christ, and how that grace forms their shared life.

Here is a simple map. Chapters 1–2 explain and defend the gospel Paul received. Chapters 3–4 explore faith, promise, the law, and inheritance. Chapters 5–6 unfold freedom, life in the Spirit, and faithful practice together. We will read the argument carefully as we go; the map is a guide rather than a substitute for the text.

The fruit of the Spirit belongs within that larger gospel of freedom. We are not beginning with a new list of characteristics we must display to prove that God accepts us. We are beginning with Jesus. From there, we will learn how the Spirit forms love, faithfulness, and a life that serves others.

Tonight we are asking: **Where do we look for the assurance that we belong to God?**

### 6:54–6:58 — Read Galatians 1:1–5 and Attend to God’s Initiative

**Read 1:1–5 aloud from the selected Bible.** Give everyone time to find the passage. Ask: “What actions in these verses belong to God and to Jesus?” Receive one or two observations from the room, then bring attention back to the passage.

Paul identifies his commission as coming from Jesus Christ and God the Father, and he names the resurrection. This letter belongs to the apostolic witness to the risen Jesus. Its authority does not rest on a teacher’s popularity or on whether a message sounds immediately reassuring.

That does not mean we stop asking questions. It means our questions bring us back to the witness God has given in Scripture. Current teachers remain accountable to that witness. Paul himself will say that a contrary message is unacceptable even if he is the one who offers it.

He then greets the churches with grace and peace from God and Jesus. Listen to where that greeting comes from. Before the churches have demonstrated that they understand everything, before Paul has completed his correction, God’s grace is already at the center of his address.

The gospel begins with God’s initiative. The Father raises Jesus. Jesus gives himself. God’s will stands behind the rescue. God receives the glory. Those actions give us something more solid than a judgment we make about ourselves by looking around the room.

### 6:58–7:04 — Jesus Gives Himself for Sinners

Verse 4 brings us to Christ’s self-giving for our sins and his rescue from the present evil age. Tim Keller emphasizes Jesus as a rescuer in *Galatians For You*. Rescue addresses a danger that a person cannot finally resolve through greater effort or a more convincing appearance.

Sin is real. It affects our relationship with God and our life with one another. Jesus does not ask us to make that problem manageable enough that we can then approach him. He gives himself for sinners. His death addresses what stands between us and God; his resurrection is part of the gospel already named in this opening.

This is substitution: Jesus acts on our behalf and bears what we could not resolve ourselves. That gives the gospel both seriousness and hope. Grace does not require us to minimize sin, and truth about sin does not require us to despair. We bring our real need to the one who gives himself for it.

The Father’s will is not presented as reluctant permission for a rescue Jesus alone desires. This rescue belongs to God’s purpose. We can attend to the Son’s self-giving and the Father’s initiative together, and receive the grace that comes from them.

Because the foundation is Christ’s action, acceptance cannot depend on how we compare with someone else. Another woman’s apparent confidence does not make Jesus less sufficient for you. Your questions do not turn his resurrection into a weaker reality. Your failure does not make his grace a reward reserved for people whose story is easier to admire.

At the same time, success does not remove the need for rescue. We can be capable, thoughtful, respected, and sincerely committed while still needing Christ. The gospel meets each person as a sinner who needs grace; it does not divide the room into those who have managed to save themselves and those who have not.

**Quiet reflection, twenty seconds:** “Look again at verse 4. What is the foundation of your hope in this passage?” Let people reflect privately without asking for a visible emotional response.

### 7:04–7:08 — Belonging without a Spiritual Comparison

**Original illustration to adapt:** Imagine entering a gathering and quietly measuring yourself against the people around you. One seems to know every answer. Another appears confident in prayer. Someone else tells a story of faithful service, while your own week feels scattered or difficult.

Learning from another person’s faith can be a gift. The pressure appears when comparison becomes a verdict on your standing before God. You begin to think that you would belong more securely if your life, your story, or your visible usefulness resembled someone else’s.

Galatians’ opening directs our attention to Christ’s work. The church is a community of people receiving his grace. We can appreciate another believer’s gifts without making those gifts a ladder of spiritual worth. We can receive encouragement without pretending our story is the same as hers.

As we begin this study together, let the gospel shape how we recognize and encourage one another. Give room for a woman to ask a genuine question, acknowledge a struggle, or receive help without being placed on a scale of comparison.

That welcome can stay truthful. If someone needs correction or a wise next step, grace does not require silence about it. We can speak truth as people who also live from Christ’s mercy. We can encourage a step of growth without making that step the price of being accepted.

One small practice is to listen before offering advice. Another is to name what the passage says about Christ before telling someone what she should do. We do not have to settle every question tonight. We can make room for faith to become deeper through attending to Jesus together.

### 7:08–7:10 — Summarize and Transition

If someone asks what Christians believe, our explanation should reach the action of God in Jesus. We can begin here: **God has acted in the crucified and risen Jesus to rescue sinners; through faith in him, we receive his grace and learn to live in the freedom he gives.**

At your tables, begin by explaining that good news to one another. Then consider how Christ’s rescue encourages your heart and how it challenges any assumption that you need to prove your worth before you can receive grace.

## Teaching Block 2 — Receive the Gospel, Recognize Its Distortions

### 7:20–7:24 — Read Galatians 1:6–9

**Read 1:6–9 aloud.** Ask participants to notice the relationship being abandoned, the altered message, and the standard applied to its messenger. Receive brief observations and continue.

Paul is alarmed by how quickly the churches are turning away from the one who calls them in grace. These are relational words. The gospel concerns knowing the living God; changing it changes the way we understand and trust him.

Keller describes gospel revision as gospel reversal. Paul says the altered message does not become another legitimate gospel. It distorts the good news the churches received.

His urgency can help us recognize how much is at stake. If someone is taught that Christ’s rescue must be completed by another condition, she can spend her life trying to secure an acceptance that the gospel invites her to receive. If someone is taught that goodness alone makes rescue unnecessary, the seriousness of sin and the hope given to sinners are both lost.

Concern for that harm belongs with care for the person. The passage does not ask us to manufacture anger or to judge how faithful someone is by how strongly she reacts. It calls us to take the truth and the people affected by it seriously.

### 7:24–7:29 — The Added Condition

In the letter’s historical setting, the gospel is being tied to requirements imposed on Gentile believers, including circumcision. Paul will examine those claims in the chapters ahead. For tonight, notice the foundation: when something is required to complete Christ’s saving work, the confidence of the believer shifts toward that addition.

We need to distinguish the rescue we receive from the life that grows from it. Learning Scripture, prayer, service, repentance, and faithful love are good. They become distorted when we make them the reason God should finally accept us.

Imagine an expectation that a faithful believer must always sound assured, always have energy to help, or always appear to be doing as well as everyone else. Different women will recognize different pressures. The question is whether one of those pressures has begun to determine our confidence before God.

If that expectation becomes a condition of acceptance, a difficult week can feel like evidence that you are no longer secure with God. You may hide what is true, overextend yourself to regain approval, or compare your inner life with another person’s outward story. The gospel gives us a different basis for trust: Christ’s work for sinners.

Consider the difference between serving because you have received grace and serving because you fear that without visible usefulness you will have no standing with God. The service itself may look similar. The source of confidence and the way you treat another person can be very different.

Received grace frees us to say both, “This responsibility matters,” and, “I need help.” It frees us to acknowledge wrong and make it right. It allows someone else’s gifts to become a cause for gratitude instead of a judgment against our own worth.

A gospel of mere virtue also offers poor hope to the person who has failed. If good people can make themselves acceptable, Christ’s rescue is reduced to something optional, and someone with a painful record may assume there is no way back. Galatians begins with Jesus acting for sinners. Repentance and trust are possible because he is sufficient.

### 7:29–7:34 — Evaluate the Message Rather than Its Appeal

Paul names even himself and an angelic messenger as accountable to the gospel already received. A compelling voice, a vivid experience, or a welcome feeling cannot make a contrary gospel true.

The biblical gospel is the standard by which the church and its teachers are judged. That gives ordinary believers a responsibility to listen carefully and test what they hear. We do not need to surrender that responsibility because someone speaks with confidence or because the message seems to offer immediate comfort.

Ask three questions of a teaching about acceptance: **What does it say Christ has accomplished? What does it make the basis of confidence before God? What must a person add in order to belong?** Bring those answers back to Scripture, including the apostolic witness we are beginning to study.

This can involve a message that sounds demanding: “You can be sure only after you achieve this.” It can also involve one that sounds generous: “Your sin does not matter, so you do not need rescue.” Grace and truth belong together. A message that leaves Christ’s saving work unnecessary cannot give the hope this passage announces.

The warning in verses 8–9 is severe. Paul invokes God’s judgment against a contrary gospel. We should hear its seriousness. We should also remember who owns that judgment. These verses are not a tool for humiliating a participant, making a table leader unquestionable, or condemning every person who holds a different view on a secondary matter.

A woman can ask a difficult question without becoming a threat to the group. A concern about a teaching can be explored patiently. We can look at what is being claimed, examine Scripture, and seek pastoral help when needed. The gospel teaches us to care for truth without placing ourselves beyond correction.

### 7:34–7:38 — Truthful Encouragement

Consider what our encouragement communicates. Do our words help someone look toward Christ, or do they imply that she must become more like another person in order to count as faithful?

When a woman shares something hard, we can acknowledge it without comparing it with someone else’s story. We can listen without treating ourselves as the measure of how she should respond. We can point toward Jesus and offer a wise next step without promising a particular emotional outcome.

We can also speak truth when an expectation has become an extra condition of acceptance. We might say, “That good responsibility is worth pursuing, but Christ’s rescue is the foundation of your hope.” Or, “You can receive help here without pretending you have a stronger story.” These are invitations to live from grace, not permission to avoid obedience or difficult repair.

**Quiet reflection, thirty seconds:** Ask yourself, “Whose approval or example most easily becomes a measure of my spiritual worth?” You do not need to name that person aloud. Then look again at what Christ does in verses 1–5. Let the passage question the measure you have been using.

Choose a small response grounded in the gospel. It may be asking for prayer, setting aside a comparison and thanking God for another person’s gift, receiving needed help, or offering an encouraging word rooted in Scripture. The practice does not earn acceptance. It is one way to inhabit the grace already offered in Christ.

### 7:38–7:40 — Transition to Tables

The church receives the gospel and remains accountable to it. Our teachers, our expectations, and the way we speak to one another all need its correcting and life-giving truth.

At the tables, identify one extra condition that can creep into a person’s confidence before God. Then discuss how we might encourage one another to return to Christ and take a faithful next step.

## Prayer, Practice, and Closing — 7:50–8:00 PM

**Practice invitation:** Reread Galatians 1:1–9 during the week. When comparison or an expectation becomes a measure of your standing with God, name it honestly in prayer and return to Christ’s action. Offer one word of truthful, grace-grounded encouragement to someone, without comparing her with another person. This invitation is not a condition of acceptance or a required report at the next gathering.

**Suggested prayer:** “Father, thank you for the grace you give us through Jesus. Help us receive his rescue with honest hearts. Where we have measured ourselves or one another by approval, comparison, or a spiritual image, bring us back to Christ. Teach us to discern what is true, to receive help, and to encourage one another with grace and truth. By your Spirit, form us in love and faithful trust. Amen.”

Communicate only actual confirmed announcements or next dates. Worship may be included within the remaining time and the offering’s actual arrangements; this draft prescribes no particular song or practice.

## Table-Leader Companion and Review

- Make room for different voices. Welcome a beginning answer and leave room to pass; quiet participation is not evidence of spiritual failure.
- Keep the passage available while discussing applications. Avoid turning someone’s personal story into a diagnosis of comparison or approval-seeking.
- If a participant hears “I must feel confident to receive grace,” return to Christ’s action. If she hears “grace means responsibility does not matter,” distinguish Christ’s sufficient rescue from the faithful response the letter develops.
- Avoid assigning a single female faith story based on marriage, motherhood, work, care, or temperament. Help each person consider the same gospel in her actual life.
- When Scripture’s authority or the severe warning raises a difficult question, listen and clarify. Arrange further pastoral conversation when appropriate rather than forcing an immediate answer.
- Significant care concerns follow the church’s existing boundaries. Do not promise absolute confidentiality or record personal disclosures in this course file.
- After actual use, tell Paul which explanations or questions were unclear, whether the timing left adequate conversation, and whether this proposed formation emphasis served the participants. Give non-identifying feedback.
