## Session Formation Emphasis

**Receive Christ’s rescue and practice honest brotherhood instead of building confidence before God on self-reliance or a personal spiritual record.** Paul reviewed the development Canvas synthesis and requested handouts from it. This pastoral emphasis remains specific to this session rather than a universal claim about male discipleship.

## Teaching Block 1 — The Gospel Is Rescue

### 6:50–6:54 — Entering the Letter

**Draft teaching script**

Galatians is a letter to real churches facing a serious question: Is what Jesus has done enough for people to belong to God, or do they need something more?

Paul writes as an apostle of Jesus Christ. These churches include Gentile believers, people who were coming into the family of God without becoming ethnically Jewish. As the letter unfolds, we will hear about circumcision, shared meals, the promise to Abraham, and the law. Those questions matter because the gospel creates a people gathered around Christ. Paul is concerned that additional requirements are being made the basis on which people can be accepted.

We need to listen carefully to that historical situation. Jewish identity is not the enemy, and God’s law is not a mistake. The question is whether Christ’s saving work is sufficient or whether we can complete it with a religious qualification of our own.

Here is a simple map for the journey. In chapters 1–2, Paul explains and defends the gospel he received. In chapters 3–4, he works through faith, promise, the law, and inheritance. In chapters 5–6, he shows the freedom and Spirit-shaped life that flow from this gospel. This map helps us follow the letter; we will keep reading the actual argument rather than treating each section as a separate slogan.

Freedom is one of the great themes of Galatians. As we go, we will discover freedom that belongs to Christ and grows into love, faithful community, and the fruit of the Spirit. The gospel reaches our thinking, our trust, and our ordinary life together.

For tonight, keep one question in mind: **What am I relying on when I think about being right with God?**

### 6:54–6:58 — Read Galatians 1:1–5 and Notice Its Center

**Read 1:1–5 aloud from the selected Bible.** Give the room time to find it. Ask people to notice the actions attributed to God and Jesus. After the reading, allow a brief observation from the room: “What does God do in these verses?” Receive one or two short responses and bring the teaching back to the text.

Paul’s opening identifies the source of his apostleship. His commission comes from Jesus Christ and God the Father, and he names the Father’s raising of Jesus from the dead. This letter comes from an apostolic witness to the risen Lord. It is not merely Paul’s private advice about how to improve ourselves.

That also gives us a boundary for teaching today. A present-day leader does not inherit Paul’s apostolic authority by being impressive or by saying that God gave him a strong experience. We listen to the apostolic witness in Scripture. Our teaching stays accountable to that witness. Later in this same passage, Paul will place even himself under the gospel’s judgment.

Notice what Paul offers these churches: grace and peace from the Father and the Lord Jesus. Even before he confronts them, he places God’s generosity before them. His concern comes from the reality of the grace they are in danger of leaving behind.

### 6:58–7:04 — What Jesus Has Done

Verse 4 gives us the heart of the opening. Jesus gives himself for our sins and delivers us from the present evil age in accordance with the Father’s will. The center of the story is his action for us.

Tim Keller describes Jesus as a rescuer in *Galatians For You*. That image matters. A person needing rescue cannot make the danger disappear by becoming more admirable. Someone must act on his behalf. Sin is a real problem in our relationship with God, and Christ comes to deal with it.

Jesus’ self-giving is personal and costly. He does not simply point from a distance toward a better life. He gives himself for sinners. This is substitution: Christ acts for us and bears the burden we could not resolve for ourselves. His death is tied to our sins, and his resurrection is already present in the opening verse. The gospel centers on the crucified and risen Jesus.

God’s initiative is visible throughout these verses. The Father raises Jesus. The Son gives himself. Rescue takes place according to the Father’s will. At the end, Paul gives glory to God. We receive grace from the one who acts; we do not become the authors of our own salvation.

That can be unsettling. If I prefer to keep control, I may want a message that lets me remain the capable person who can sort everything out. But rescue asks me to admit that I need what I cannot produce. Grace meets that need with Christ’s sufficient work.

It is also deeply hopeful. A failure does not place a person beyond the reach of Jesus’ grace. A successful life does not place someone beyond the need for it. The gospel brings both the person proud of his record and the person ashamed of it to the same rescuer.

**Pause for twenty seconds:** “Look again at verse 4. What does Jesus do that you cannot do for yourself?” Keep this as quiet reflection inside the teaching time, not a second table discussion.

### 7:04–7:08 — Receiving Grace in Our Actual Lives

**Original illustration to adapt:** Imagine assembling a case for why God should accept you. You include work you have done well, people you have helped, responsibilities you have carried, and spiritual habits you have kept. Then you notice the failures. You promise that next week you will build a stronger case.

Responsible work, care for others, and spiritual disciplines are good. The problem comes when they become the foundation of your confidence before God. Then every failure threatens your standing, and every success gives you a reason to compare yourself with someone else. You are continually presenting your own record as evidence that you should be accepted.

Galatians begins elsewhere. It begins with Jesus giving himself for sinners. His work supplies the confidence that your record cannot sustain. From that received grace, we can repent, learn, keep commitments, and serve. We do those things as people who need and receive Christ, rather than as people trying to make his rescue unnecessary.

As we begin this study together, let the gospel make room for honest brotherhood. Some of us may find it easier to offer help than to receive it. Some may find it easier to sound certain than to admit a question. If your acceptance rests in Christ, you can acknowledge need without turning it into a verdict on your worth.

That does not mean telling everyone everything. It can begin with one truthful sentence to a trustworthy brother: “I need prayer here,” or “I am struggling to trust God in this,” or “I need help keeping this commitment.” Receiving help is one way we live as people whose hope rests in Jesus rather than in being self-sufficient.

### 7:08–7:10 — Summarize and Transition

If someone asked you what the gospel is, would your explanation begin with the good things Christians should do, or with what God has done through Jesus?

We can begin here: **God has acted in the crucified and risen Jesus to rescue sinners; through faith in him, we receive his grace and learn to live in the freedom he gives.** The rest of Galatians will help us understand that more fully.

At your tables, begin with that gospel question. Then consider what receiving grace would look like in the part of life where you most want to prove that you can manage on your own.

## Teaching Block 2 — The Gospel We Receive Must Be Guarded

### 7:20–7:24 — Read Galatians 1:6–9

**Read 1:6–9 aloud.** Ask the room to notice what the Galatians are turning from, what is being offered instead, and who is accountable to the gospel. Receive brief observations, then continue.

Paul’s tone changes sharply. He is alarmed. This is more than a disagreement about an interesting idea. People are turning away from the God who calls them in Christ’s grace. The truth of the gospel concerns a relationship with the living God.

Their teachers are presenting a changed message. Paul insists that this does not amount to a second equally valid gospel. It distorts the one that brought them into the life of grace.

This helps explain his emotion. Imagine seeing someone offered a way of belonging that promises security while drawing them away from the only rescuer who can give it. Care for the person and concern for the truth belong together. Our aim in learning this passage is to grow in that care, not to borrow Paul’s intensity as an excuse for harshness.

### 7:24–7:29 — When an Addition Changes the Foundation

We can describe the problem as “faith plus.” As the letter unfolds, Paul addresses requirements such as circumcision being imposed on Gentile believers. The requirement is being made part of what they must have to be accepted as righteous before God. Adding a condition to Christ’s sufficient work changes the foundation on which they stand.

The same danger can appear in different forms today. We might say that Jesus saves, but quietly make our confidence depend on being the dependable man, having a spotless spiritual record, belonging to the right circle, or never letting anyone see a weakness.

Different people will recognize different pressures. The question is what happens when we make any achievement or identity do the saving work that belongs to Christ.

Consider two sentences. “Because I have received grace in Christ, I want to become faithful in this responsibility.” And: “Unless I do this responsibility perfectly, I cannot be sure God will accept me.” The outward activity may look similar. The source of confidence is different.

Grace does not remove responsibility or make repentance unnecessary. It gives us a different place from which to obey. A man can acknowledge wrong, seek forgiveness, accept correction, and make restitution because he is trusting Christ. He does not need to deny the wrong in order to protect a case he is building for his own worth.

The gospel also gives hope to someone whose record is painful. If acceptance is finally secured by our virtue, the person who has failed badly has little hope. If Jesus truly rescues sinners, repentance and a new beginning are real possibilities. We will keep seeing that power of grace throughout this study.

### 7:29–7:34 — Test the Message, Even When the Messenger Impresses You

Verses 8–9 apply the standard even to Paul and to an angelic messenger. Impressive credentials do not make a different gospel true. Nor does a strong personality, a moving experience, or the popularity of a teaching.

The church itself is judged by the biblical gospel. That means Scripture can correct the teacher and the group as well as the individual. We do not protect a leader from appropriate testing by saying, “He seems sincere,” or “He has done good things,” or “He speaks confidently.” Sincerity, service, and confidence are not substitutes for fidelity to Christ’s gospel.

For any teaching about acceptance before God, ask: **What does it say Christ has done? What does it make the basis of our confidence? What does it require us to add in order to belong?** Bring the answer back to the text and to the broader apostolic witness.

The warning about a contrary gospel is serious. Paul invokes God’s judgment. We should not soften that into “truth does not really matter.” We should also not turn it into permission to curse a participant, attack another church, or treat every unanswered question as rebellion. A question may be the very place where someone is learning to hear the gospel more clearly.

At these tables, we can care about the truth and listen patiently. We can identify a harmful claim without shaming the person who has believed it. We can say, “Let us look at the passage together,” rather than putting ourselves above the need for correction.

### 7:34–7:38 — Grace-Grounded Brotherhood

Here is a test close to home: Do the ways we encourage one another help a brother trust Christ, or make him feel that his spiritual worth depends on appearing capable?

If someone admits a struggle, immediately prescribing more effort may miss what he first needs to hear. We can acknowledge the seriousness of the struggle and point him toward the grace of Jesus. From there we can help him take a wise next step. Grace and truth remain together.

Likewise, when we speak about growth, we do not need to pretend that effort is bad. Prayer, keeping a promise, attending to Scripture, asking for help, and putting a harmful habit to death all matter. The gospel teaches us to pursue that growth through Christ and the Spirit rather than using it to buy acceptance.

**Quiet reflection, thirty seconds:** Complete this sentence privately: “When I feel that I have failed, I try to regain my standing by ______.” Then ask whether that response helps you receive Christ or pushes you back toward self-justification.

Choose an ordinary next step. You might ask a trusted believer to pray with you, confess a wrong and begin making it right, or open this passage again when you find yourself constructing a spiritual scorecard. The action is a response to grace; its success is not a new requirement for belonging to God.

### 7:38–7:40 — Transition to Tables

We are learning to receive a gospel we did not create. Christ is sufficient. The church’s teaching, our own assumptions, and our habits of encouraging one another all stay accountable to that gospel.

Discuss one possible addition that can creep into your confidence before God. Then consider one way your table can help a man move from hiding or self-justifying toward Christ and a faithful response.

## Prayer, Practice, and Closing — 7:50–8:00 PM

**Practice invitation:** During the week, reread Galatians 1:1–9. When you notice yourself presenting your record as the reason God should accept you, name it honestly and return to Christ’s action for you. If appropriate, ask a trusted brother for prayer and help with one faithful next step. This is an invitation, not a condition of acceptance or a required report next time.

**Suggested prayer:** “Father, thank you for the grace you give us through Jesus. We bring you our pride, our failures, and our attempts to make ourselves secure. Teach us to receive Christ’s rescue, to turn from what is false, and to speak truth with love. By your Spirit, form us into men who can receive help and encourage others faithfully. Keep our confidence in Jesus. Amen.”

Use the remaining time for actual confirmed announcements. Do not supply an unconfirmed next date. Worship may be included if it fits the available time and the offering’s actual arrangements; no song is prescribed by this draft.

## Table-Leader Companion and Review

- Keep the first question connected to the passage. Let several men contribute before offering your summary.
- Offer room to pass; silence is not evidence of resistance or immaturity. Do not make a particular emotional response the measure of grace.
- If someone hears “grace means my actions do not matter,” distinguish acceptance through Christ from the faithful response the letter will develop. If someone hears “I must admit a struggle to belong,” remove that added condition immediately.
- If someone objects to apostolic authority or worries about the curse, listen and clarify the passage. An unresolved question can become a later pastoral conversation.
- Significant care concerns follow the church’s existing pastoral boundaries. Do not record personal disclosures in this course file or promise absolute confidentiality.
