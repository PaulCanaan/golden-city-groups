## Purpose

Name why a ministry made of separate rooms has to work deliberately at connection — among its leaders, and between the church and the groups it launches.

## Leaders Serve Together Without Serving Together

Group leaders serve together for the body of the church. They do not literally serve together.

A City Group leader leads in their own living room, on their own night, with their own people. A Collective co-lead and a Bible Club facilitator are doing parallel work in different rooms, often at the same hour. They belong to one ministry team that almost never sees one another work.

That is not a flaw in the model. It is the shape of the model, and it has consequences worth naming plainly:

- **Isolation is the role's default condition, not a failure of character.** A leader who feels alone in it is experiencing the structure working as designed, unless something is built to counteract it.
- **Nobody sees a leader lead.** The people in the room see the group; they rarely see what the evening cost the person hosting it.
- **Every leader re-solves the same problems privately.** One leader is working out how to handle a dominant talker, another how to restart a conversation that died, another whether to text the person who has missed three weeks. None of them knows the others are doing it.
- **Encouragement has no natural source.** In most serving roles, teammates supply it. Here there are no teammates in the room.

So connecting with, serving, and supporting other group leaders is not a nice addition to the role. It is how the role becomes survivable — and the ministry has to provide it, because it will not happen on its own.

### Leader resources

Resources exist so that a leader never has to invent hospitality, prayer, conflict handling, or Scripture-centered conversation from scratch at nine o'clock on a Tuesday. Their purpose is to remove unnecessary weight, not to standardize every room.

### CoJourner

CoJourner is the selected training program, four sessions on a biweekly cadence over roughly two months. Its value is not only the content. A cohort means leaders are formed *alongside* other leaders rather than briefed individually — the first structural answer to isolation that the ministry has.

CoJourner does not replace role-specific preparation, safeguarding, assessment, appointment, or readiness approval.

### Group leader collaborations

**Approved under DEC-056; the first one is Saturday evening, November 7, 2026, a dinner hosted by Paul.** A collaboration is a space where group leaders share experience and learn from one another — not training, and not a check-in with the Groups Director. Only a collaboration produces knowledge the ministry did not already have, and only there does a leader discover that their hardest month is not unique to them.

Its purpose, how it differs from training and check-ins, its rhythm, boundaries, and gathering records live in [[05 Leadership/Group Leader Collaboration/About Collaboration|About Group Leader Collaboration]].

The Groups Ministry Director is also available to leaders pastorally, not only operationally. Following Russ's practice, Paul will set up a sign-up calendar so any leader can book time with him for support (DEC-064).

## Why Consistency and Longevity Are the Real Struggle

Two struggles are common to small church group ministries, and naming them early is better than being surprised by them:

- **Consistency of attendees.** People come irregularly. A group of ten on paper is six on a given week, and rarely the same six.
- **Longevity of groups.** Groups fade after a season. Energy is high at launch, thins through the middle, and quietly stops.

Neither is evidence of a bad leader or a bad group. Both are predictable, and a group that quietly ends usually did not fail in its final month — it ran out of support long before, and nobody was close enough to see it.

That is the argument for the church and the ministry staying connected to the groups rather than only launching them. **A ministry that launches well and supports poorly will have to launch again every season.**

**One boundary.** Consistency matters because sustained formation requires it — people are not formed in a room they rarely enter. It does not become the measure of health. A full roster and high attendance prove nothing on their own, and ministry health is not reduced to attendance or activity. Watch consistency the way you watch a symptom: as something that tells you where to look.

## What This Requires of the Ministry

- **Launch nothing the ministry is not prepared to support.** Support capacity is part of readiness, not something arranged afterward.
- **Treat support as a standing commitment, not a launch-week service.** The [[07 Operations/Workflows/Leader Support Check-In|Leader Support Check-In]] carries the working rhythm: connection before the first meeting, a check-in after it, periodic contact through the season, timely response when a leader asks, and an end-of-season review.
- **Know leaders as people.** A leader should be known, prayed for, supported, corrected where necessary, and connected to pastoral help — without support becoming task surveillance.
- **Notice a struggling group early**, while there is still something to support.
- **Build the connection leaders cannot build for themselves.** They are in different rooms on different nights; only the ministry can put them in the same one.
