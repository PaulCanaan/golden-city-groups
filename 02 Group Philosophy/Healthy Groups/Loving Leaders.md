# Loving Leaders

## Document Status

**Draft ministry philosophy.** Healthy leaders make healthy groups possible, so this page describes how the ministry cares for the people carrying its groups. It interprets confirmed direction — DEC-027's minimum standard, DEC-028's care boundaries, DEC-033's escalation baseline — and does not add policy. No coaching layer is established, and the support cadence remains an operational decision rather than approved policy.

The external research cited below informs this page. It does not govern it.

## Purpose

Name what it actually costs to lead a group, and what the ministry owes the person paying it.

## Why This Comes First

A group cannot stay healthier than the person carrying it for very long.

Not because the leader is the source of the group's life — the Holy Spirit is, and no leader can produce formation by effort. But a depleted leader stops being able to do the ordinary things that make a healthy room possible. They stop noticing who is quiet. They stop preparing. They stop welcoming well. They keep showing up while having nothing left to give, and the room slowly becomes a meeting.

So loving leaders is not a kindness the ministry adds once the important work is done. It is upstream of everything else in this folder.

## What a Leader Is Actually Carrying

Most of it is invisible to the people in the room:

- the late-night text from someone in crisis
- the weekly preparation nobody sees
- the pastoral conversations that do not end when the evening does
- the person who stopped coming, and the question of whether to reach out again
- the quiet pressure to be spiritually strong for everyone else, on a week when they are not
- the absorbed weight of other people's burdens, carried as though they were the leader's to solve

Ministry research is consistent on the last point: leaders burn out when they take on the burdens of the people in the group, and **burnout is usually a systems problem rather than a character flaw** — roles with no boundaries, no defined end, and no one asking how the leader is.

A ministry that knows this and does nothing about it has decided to spend its leaders.

## Six Signs to Watch For

Lifeway Research names six signs that a group leader is burning out. They are worth knowing by heart, because a leader will rarely announce any of them:

1. **They use God to run from God** — filling life with Christian activity to avoid what is actually going on.
2. **They ignore anger, sadness, and fear** — bottling emotion to appear strong for the group.
3. **They die to the wrong things** — giving up what genuinely restores them, and feeling guilty for wanting it.
4. **They *do for* God instead of *being with* him** — ministry work displacing the leader's own communion with Christ.
5. **They spiritualize away conflict** — avoiding the hard conversation until the problem is larger than the conversation would have been.
6. **They live without limits** — absorbing the group's burdens as though they alone must resolve them.

Three of these — the first, second, and fifth — are [[Emotional Bypass vs. Spiritual Bypass|spiritual bypass]] appearing in the leader rather than in the room. A leader who is bypassing their own heart will eventually build a group that bypasses everyone else's.

## What Loving a Leader Means Here

**Know them as a person, not a role.** The [[06 Resources/Leader Development/Leader Support Check-In|Leader Support Check-In]] asks how the leader is personally and spiritually *before* it asks how the group is going. That order is deliberate and should not be reversed for efficiency.

**Ask the question that is hard to volunteer.** *Where do you feel stretched, tired, uncertain, or alone?* Leaders rarely raise this unprompted, because raising it feels like admitting the group is not working.

**Give explicit permission.** To rest. To be imperfect. To not have an answer. To say that a week was hard. To enjoy what restores them without guilt. Permission that is never said out loud is not permission.

**Define the season.** Research is blunt here: many group roles are designed for a short season and then extended indefinitely, and a defined season gives permission to serve well rather than endlessly. An end-of-season review and prayer conversation is part of the rhythm, not an exit interview.

**Do not let a leader carry what is not theirs.** Under DEC-028, leaders offer prayer, presence, practical care, and pastoral connection — they do not act as sole crisis responders or licensed counselors. Under DEC-033, significant pastoral or spiritual-care concerns are escalated rather than left with the group leader as the only responder. Enforcing that boundary *is* an act of love, and the ministry, not the leader, is responsible for enforcing it.

**Put leaders with other leaders.** They serve together without serving together; the ministry has to build that connection deliberately. See [[Supporting Leaders and Groups]].

**Love includes correction.** A leader who is drifting, overreaching, or quietly harming the room is not served by silence. Correction within the approved process is care, not its opposite.

## Guardrails

- **Support is not surveillance.** A check-in that only audits attendance reporting and curriculum progress teaches a leader that the ministry cares about the group's output, not the leader.
- **Care is not a performance review**, and it should not be scheduled only when something is wrong.
- **The ministry cannot love leaders it does not know.** This is why relational connection before the first meeting is part of the rhythm rather than an optional courtesy.
- **Noticing is not diagnosing.** Watching for the signs above is pastoral attention; it does not make a ministry leader a counselor, and serious concerns follow the approved pastoral path.
